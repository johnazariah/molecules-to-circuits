Chapter 3: From Spatial to Spin-Orbital Integrals

Centre for Quantum Software & Information, University of Technology Sydney

Every electron has a spin. Every integral must account for it. This chapter doubles the index space and fills in the complete integral tables that drive the encoding pipeline.

In This Chapter¶

  • What you’ll learn: How to expand spatial-orbital integrals into spin-orbital one-body and two-body terms, including cross-spin interactions that many implementations forget.

  • Why this matters: The encoding step operates on spin-orbital creation and annihilation operators. Missing a spin block changes both repulsion energies and configuration couplings; an otherwise correct encoder cannot repair that input.

  • Prerequisites: Chapters 1–2 (you know the spatial integrals and the notation conventions).

Why We Need Spin-Orbitals¶

In Chapter 1, we worked with spatial orbitals — the molecular orbitals σg\sigma_gσg​ and σu\sigma_uσu​, described by their spatial wavefunctions. There were two of them, and the integrals were 2×22 \times 22×2 matrices and 2×2×2×22 \times 2 \times 2 \times 22×2×2×2 tensors.

But an electron is not just a spatial wavefunction. It also has spin.

What spin is (and isn’t)¶

Electron spin is intrinsic angular momentum, not the rotation of a small charged sphere. Its spin quantum number is s=1/2s=1/2s=1/2; its angular-momentum magnitude is ℏs(s+1)\hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)}ℏs(s+1)​. A measurement of the projection along a chosen axis has two possible outcomes.

For a spin-1/21/21/2 particle, the projection along the zzz-axis can take only two values:

  • ms=+1/2m_s = +1/2ms​=+1/2, which we call α\alphaα or “spin-up” (↑\uparrow↑)

  • ms=−1/2m_s = -1/2ms​=−1/2, which we call β\betaβ or “spin-down” (↓\downarrow↓)

The two possible projections follow from spin-1/21/21/2 angular momentum. The chosen axis and the names α,β\alpha,\betaα,β are conventions. These two orthonormal vectors span the spin state space; an arbitrary spin state can also be their superposition. It has the same dimension as a qubit, though the qubits in our encoding store molecular occupation information, not individual electron spins.

Why spin doubles the orbital space¶

The Pauli exclusion principle says that no two electrons can occupy exactly the same quantum state. A quantum state is specified by both the spatial orbital and the spin. So the spatial orbital σg\sigma_gσg​ can hold:

  • one electron with spin α\alphaα (the spin-orbital σg,α\sigma_g, \alphaσg​,α)

  • one electron with spin β\betaβ (the spin-orbital σg,β\sigma_g, \betaσg​,β)

  • but NOT two electrons with the same spin

Each spatial orbital produces two spin-orbitals. Two spatial orbitals give four spin-orbitals. Seven spatial orbitals give fourteen. Doubling each index makes a one-body array four times larger and a four-index two-body array sixteen times larger. Only four of its sixteen spin blocks can survive the spin selection rules. Storage slots and allowed entries are different counts.

Spin-orbit coupling: when spin and space talk to each other¶

The non-relativistic Coulomb Hamiltonian we use has no explicit spin operator. The one-body expectation in σgα\sigma_g\alphaσg​α therefore equals that in σgβ\sigma_g\betaσg​β. Antisymmetry still links spatial and spin structure in the many-electron state: singlet and triplet electronic states need not have the same energy. Spin independence of the operator does not erase exchange.

This is an approximation. In reality, spin-orbit coupling — the interaction between an electron’s spin angular momentum and its orbital angular momentum — mixes the spin and spatial degrees of freedom. Hydrogenic estimates often scale roughly as Z4Z^4Z4, but molecular trends also depend on electronic structure and bonding. The effect is negligible for H₂ and small for the light-element examples in this book, but it can be significant for heavy atoms such as iodine, lead, and the actinides.

For the molecules in this book (H₂, H₂O), spin-orbit coupling is negligible and we work with the non-relativistic Hamiltonian throughout. This means:

  • One-body integrals are diagonal in spin: ⟨α∣β⟩=0\langle \alpha \mid \beta \rangle = 0⟨α∣β⟩=0

  • Two-body integrals conserve each electron’s spin independently

  • The Hamiltonian commutes with total spin squared and spin projection, so eigenstates can be chosen with definite SSS and MSM_SMS​

When spin-orbit coupling matters (heavy-element chemistry, relativistic quantum chemistry), the spin-orbital structure becomes more complex — spin is no longer a good quantum number, and the integral expansion rules in this chapter must be modified. We won’t need that here, but it’s worth knowing the boundary of our approximation.

The upshot for encoding¶

The second-quantized Hamiltonian

sums over spin-orbital indices p,q,r,sp, q, r, sp,q,r,s. The creation operator ap†a_p^\daggerap†​ creates an electron in spin-orbital ppp — a specific spatial orbital with a specific spin. This means we need integral tables indexed by spin-orbitals, not spatial orbitals.

For H₂: 2 spatial orbitals → 4 spin-orbitals. The one-body integral matrix goes from 2×22 \times 22×2 to 4×44 \times 44×4. The two-body integral tensor goes from 24=162^4 = 1624=16 elements to 44=2564^4 = 25644=256 elements (most of which are zero).

Each spatial orbital μ\muμ gives rise to two spin-orbitals. We use interleaved indexing — alternating α\alphaα and β\betaβ for each spatial orbital:

The conversion between spin-orbital index ppp and spatial orbital/spin:

The floor brackets mean integer division rounded down. For p=3p=3p=3, ⌊3/2⌋=1\lfloor3/2\rfloor=1⌊3/2⌋=1 and 3 mod 2=13\bmod2=13mod2=1, giving spatial orbital 1 with β\betaβ spin. The value 3/2=1.53/2=1.53/2=1.5 is not a possible array index. In the opposite direction, spatial orbital μ\muμ and spin bit σ\sigmaσ give p=2μ+σp=2\mu+\sigmap=2μ+σ.

This book’s PySCF-to-FockMap conversion imposes interleaved indexing; PySCF itself does not define one universal spin-orbital ordering for every interface. If your integral source uses a different order, permute the indices before proceeding. This is another silent-error source.

One-Body Expansion¶

Write the spin-orbital as an actual product function:

ψp(r,s)=ϕμp(r)ωσp(s),μp=⌊p/2⌋,σp=p mod 2.\psi_p(\mathbf r,s) =\phi_{\mu_p}(\mathbf r)\omega_{\sigma_p}(s),\qquad \mu_p=\lfloor p/2\rfloor,\quad \sigma_p=p\bmod2.ψp​(r,s)=ϕμp​​(r)ωσp​​(s),μp​=⌊p/2⌋,σp​=pmod2.

(4)

The spin-orbital one-body integral is straightforward: the spatial integral survives only when the spins match.

hpqspin=h⌊p/2⌋, ⌊q/2⌋spatial×δ(σp,σq)h^{\text{spin}}_{pq} = h^{\text{spatial}}_{\lfloor p/2 \rfloor,\, \lfloor q/2 \rfloor} \times \delta(\sigma_p, \sigma_q)hpqspin​=h⌊p/2⌋,⌊q/2⌋spatial​×δ(σp​,σq​)

In words: the one-body integral between spin-orbitals ppp and qqq equals the spatial integral between their parent spatial orbitals, provided they have the same spin. If the spins differ, the integral is zero.

Why? The one-body Hamiltonian (kinetic energy + electron-nucleus attraction) does not act on spin. The spin part of the wavefunction integrates to ⟨α∣α⟩=1\langle \alpha \mid \alpha \rangle = 1⟨α∣α⟩=1 or ⟨β∣β⟩=1\langle \beta \mid \beta \rangle = 1⟨β∣β⟩=1 for same spins, and ⟨α∣β⟩=0\langle \alpha \mid \beta \rangle = 0⟨α∣β⟩=0 for opposite spins.

Two-Body Expansion¶

The spin-orbital two-body integral in physicist’s notation is:

⟨pq∣rs⟩spin=[⌊p/2⌋ ⌊r/2⌋ ∣ ⌊q/2⌋ ⌊s/2⌋]spatialδσpσrδσqσs.\langle pq \mid rs\rangle_{\text{spin}} =\left[\lfloor p/2\rfloor\,\lfloor r/2\rfloor \,\big|\,\lfloor q/2\rfloor\,\lfloor s/2\rfloor\right]_{\text{spatial}} \delta_{\sigma_p\sigma_r}\delta_{\sigma_q\sigma_s}.⟨pq∣rs⟩spin​=[⌊p/2⌋⌊r/2⌋∣∣​⌊q/2⌋⌊s/2⌋]spatial​δσp​σr​​δσq​σs​​.
(7)

where we’ve used the conversion ⟨pq∣rs⟩=[pr∣qs]\langle pq \mid rs\rangle = [pr \mid qs]⟨pq∣rs⟩=[pr∣qs] from Chapter 2 to express the result in terms of chemist’s spatial integrals.

The derivation is the same separation as for one-body integrals, twice: the spin-coordinate sum at electron coordinate 1 is ∑s1ωσp∗(s1)ωσr(s1)\sum_{s_1}\omega_{\sigma_p}^*(s_1)\omega_{\sigma_r}(s_1)∑s1​​ωσp​∗​(s1​)ωσr​​(s1​); the sum at coordinate 2 is ∑s2ωσq∗(s2)ωσs(s2)\sum_{s_2}\omega_{\sigma_q}^*(s_2)\omega_{\sigma_s}(s_2)∑s2​​ωσq​∗​(s2​)ωσs​​(s2​). The Coulomb kernel 1/r121/r_{12}1/r12​ depends on neither spin coordinate. It therefore stays inside the spatial integral while the two spin overlaps become the two deltas.

The key insight: each electron independently conserves its spin. Electron 1 (described by indices ppp and rrr) must have the same spin in both orbitals. Electron 2 (indices qqq and sss) must also conserve its spin. But the two electrons need not have the same spin as each other.

This means there are four spin-allowed blocks. Spatial symmetry or other selection rules may still make entries within them zero:

All four blocks contribute. The cross-spin blocks (αβ\alpha\betaαβ and βα\beta\alphaβα) are the ones that beginners most commonly forget.

Common Mistake #1: Omitting cross-spin integrals. This was our first-implementation bug, and it changes the Hamiltonian, not just the quality of its solution. In this particular H₂ basis it removes the opposite-spin configuration couplings and the bonding pair’s Coulomb repulsion. In larger systems same-spin terms can still be off-diagonal. Neither “missing cross-spin” nor “diagonal in an occupation basis” is a general synonym for Hartree–Fock.

Worked example: a cross-spin integral¶

Consider ⟨0α, 1β∣0α, 1β⟩\langle 0\alpha,\, 1\beta \mid 0\alpha,\, 1\beta \rangle⟨0α,1β∣0α,1β⟩. In our index scheme: p=0,q=3,r=0,s=3p = 0, q = 3, r = 0, s = 3p=0,q=3,r=0,s=3.

Check spin conservation:

  • Electron 1: σp=α\sigma_p = \alphaσp​=α, σr=α\sigma_r = \alphaσr​=α → same spin ✓

  • Electron 2: σq=β\sigma_q = \betaσq​=β, σs=β\sigma_s = \betaσs​=β → same spin ✓

Spatial indices: ⌊p/2⌋=0\lfloor p/2 \rfloor = 0⌊p/2⌋=0, ⌊q/2⌋=1\lfloor q/2 \rfloor = 1⌊q/2⌋=1, ⌊r/2⌋=0\lfloor r/2 \rfloor = 0⌊r/2⌋=0, ⌊s/2⌋=1\lfloor s/2 \rfloor = 1⌊s/2⌋=1.

Convert to chemist’s spatial: [⌊p/2⌋⌊r/2⌋∣⌊q/2⌋⌊s/2⌋]=[00∣11]=0.6637114014[\lfloor p/2 \rfloor \lfloor r/2 \rfloor \mid \lfloor q/2 \rfloor \lfloor s/2 \rfloor] = [00 \mid 11] = 0.6637114014[⌊p/2⌋⌊r/2⌋∣⌊q/2⌋⌊s/2⌋]=[00∣11]=0.6637114014 Ha.

This integral represents the Coulomb repulsion between a spin-α\alphaα electron in σg\sigma_gσg​ and a spin-β\betaβ electron in σu\sigma_uσu​. It has nothing to do with spin flips — both electrons keep their original spins — but it is a cross-spin interaction because the two electrons have different spins.

A coupling entry and a forbidden neighbour¶

Now request ⟨01∣23⟩spin\langle01|23\rangle_{\mathrm{spin}}⟨01∣23⟩spin​. At coordinate 1, indices 0 and 2 are both α\alphaα; at coordinate 2, indices 1 and 3 are both β\betaβ. The spatial lookup is [01∣01][01|01][01∣01], so the value is 0.1812104620 Ha. Its operator removes modes 2 and 3 and creates modes 0 and 1: a pair moves from the antibonding MO to the bonding MO.

Swap only its two ket indices and request ⟨01∣32⟩spin\langle01|32\rangle_{\mathrm{spin}}⟨01∣32⟩spin​. Both deltas now vanish: mode 0 is α\alphaα but mode 3 is β\betaβ, and mode 1 is β\betaβ but mode 2 is α\alphaα. The spatial lookup still has a nonzero value; the spin-orbital integral is zero. Expanding a spatial tensor is not copying every value into every spin slot.

For a fixed spatial quartet, there are 24=162^4=1624=16 assignments of four spin bits. The two independent equality constraints leave 22=42^2=422=4 assignments. That is why the dense tensor grows sixteenfold but the spin-allowed part grows fourfold.

With seven spatial orbitals, water has 196 dense one-body slots but at most 98 spin-allowed entries; it has 38,416 dense two-body slots but at most 9,604 spin-allowed entries. These are upper bounds before spatial symmetry and numerical zeros, not measured nonzero counts for a water geometry. A minimal basis does not force every allowed entry to be nonzero.

For our H₂ table, two spatial one-body entries and eight spatial two-body entries are nonzero. The eight two-body entries come from 1+1+2+41+1+2+41+1+2+4 positions in the table below. Each expands into four spin assignments, giving 32 raw two-body entries, of which 16 are cross-spin. There are only four distinct nonzero spatial two-body values. Counting values, positions, and spin blocks gives different answers because they are different questions.

Finally, a nonzero integral need not yield a nonzero operator monomial. For example, ⟨00∣00⟩spin\langle00|00\rangle_{\mathrm{spin}}⟨00∣00⟩spin​ with all four indices referring to the same spin-orbital can be nonzero as an integral, but its factor (a0†)2a02(a_0^\dagger)^2a_0^2(a0†​)2a02​ vanishes by exclusion. Keeping such raw entries is consistent with the integral contract; the operator algebra removes them. Do not pre-emptively replace the raw tensor by antisymmetrised or collected coefficients and feed it to a builder that expects raw entries.

Before we can encode, we need to understand the target. Chapter 4 introduces the quantum computer’s native operations — the language into which we’ll translate everything. Without it, phrases like “Pauli weight” and “CNOT cost” are empty.

Key Takeaways¶

  • Each spatial orbital produces two spin-orbitals (α\alphaα and β\betaβ). The spin-orbital index space is twice the spatial index space.

  • One-body integrals require same-spin: hpqspin=h⌊p/2⌋,⌊q/2⌋spatial×δ(σp,σq)h^{\text{spin}}_{pq} = h^{\text{spatial}}_{\lfloor p/2 \rfloor, \lfloor q/2 \rfloor} \times \delta(\sigma_p, \sigma_q)hpqspin​=h⌊p/2⌋,⌊q/2⌋spatial​×δ(σp​,σq​).

  • Two-body integrals require each electron to independently conserve spin: δ(σp,σr)×δ(σq,σs)\delta(\sigma_p, \sigma_r) \times \delta(\sigma_q, \sigma_s)δ(σp​,σr​)×δ(σq​,σs​). This admits four spin blocks, including the cross-spin blocks αβ\alpha\betaαβ and βα\beta\alphaβα.

  • Omitting cross-spin integrals removes real interactions. In this H₂ example the HF determinant itself then has the wrong repulsion energy; this is not a Hartree–Fock approximation.

  • FockMap expects spin-orbital integrals in physicist’s convention with interleaved indexing.

Common Mistakes¶

  1. Omitting cross-spin blocks. The αβ\alpha\betaαβ and βα\beta\alphaβα blocks contribute the expected configuration-coupling Pauli terms. Omitting them produces the wrong fermionic matrix and fails the independent FCI reference. If those terms are absent, check the cross-spin expansion first.

  2. Wrong spin-orbital ordering. Interleaved (0α,0β,1α,1β0\alpha, 0\beta, 1\alpha, 1\beta0α,0β,1α,1β) vs blocked (0α,1α,0β,1β0\alpha, 1\alpha, 0\beta, 1\beta0α,1α,0β,1β) indexing produces different integral tables. The eigenvalues are independent of indexing, but the Pauli string structure changes. Always check your convention.

  3. Spatial-to-physicist conversion errors. This chapter combines two index transformations: spatial→spin-orbital and chemist→physicist. It’s easy to get one right and the other wrong. Use the dual-convention table above as a cross-check.

Exercises¶

  1. Cross-spin counting. How many non-zero spin-orbital two-body integrals does H₂/STO-3G have in total? How many of those are cross-spin? (Answer: 32 total, 16 cross-spin — exactly half.)

  2. Missing block detection. Evaluate the canonical HF determinant with and without its opposite-spin Coulomb repulsion. Add VnnV_{nn}Vnn​ in both cases. Explain why the lower result from the damaged tensor is not a better variational solution of the original Hamiltonian.

  3. Blocked indexing. Rewrite the one-body spin-orbital table using blocked indexing (0α,1α,0β,1β0\alpha, 1\alpha, 0\beta, 1\beta0α,1α,0β,1β → indices 0, 1, 2, 3). Which entries of the matrix change? Do the eigenvalues change?

  4. H₂O scale-up. The all-electron water model has 7 spatial orbitals and 14 spin-orbitals. Compute dense slots and spin-allowed slots separately for both tensors. Can either count alone tell you how many entries are actually nonzero?

  5. Allowed is not nonzero. Evaluate the spin deltas and spatial keys for ⟨01∣23⟩\langle01|23\rangle⟨01∣23⟩, ⟨01∣32⟩\langle01|32\rangle⟨01∣32⟩ and ⟨00∣00⟩\langle00|00\rangle⟨00∣00⟩, with all indices now spin-orbital indices. Which corresponding operator monomial vanishes even though its raw integral is nonzero?

  6. Spin projection is not total spin. List the four H₂ determinants with MS=0M_S=0MS​=0 from Chapter 1. Explain why keeping equal numbers of α\alphaα and β\betaβ electrons alone does not select only singlets.
